	\section*{Notes}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \alert{Amount of water:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \alert{Rice cooker:} use a 1:1 ratio (equal cups rice and water)
			\item \alert{Stovetop:} use about 1 : 1.1–1.2 (10–20\% more water than rice)
		\end{itemize}
		% \item \alert{Amount of Nori Sheets:}
		% \begin{itemize}
		% 	\item \alert{Hosomaki (thin roll):} half a nori sheet.
		% 	\item \alert{Futomaki (thick roll):} a full nori sheet.
		% 	\item \alert{Uramaki (inside-out roll):} usually half a nori sheet
		% \end{itemize}
		\item \alert{Hosomaki Technique:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Standard nori sheets are about 19 × 21 cm. Always cut across the longer side (so end up with two rectangles about 19 × 10.5 cm). This way, the sheet is wide enough to roll properly, while the height stays proportioned for hosomaki or uramaki
			\item Shiny/smooth side down, rough side up
			\item Spread a thin rice layer, leaving about 1cm uncovered at the top edge for sealing; just enough to cover the nori
			\item Place the filling in a thin line across the rice, about 1–2 cm from the bottom edge
			\item Roll tightly from the bottom up
			\item Slice with a sharp, damp knife into 8 even pieces (always half them); if the knife is not sharp, gently saw back and forth without pressing
		\end{itemize}
		\item \alert{Futomaki Technique:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item See Hosomaki Technique
			\item Full nori sheet without cutting
			\item Spread a thicker rice layer over the sheet, leaving about 1–2 cm uncovered at the top edge for sealing
			\item Arrange several fillings in a line across the rice about 2–3 cm from the bottom edge
			% \item Keep fillings balanced, futomaki usually contains 3–5 ingredients
			\item Roll tightly from the bottom up
			\item Slice with a sharp, damp knife into 6 even pieces
		\end{itemize}
		\item \alert{Uramaki Technique:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item see Hosomaki Technique
			\item Spread an even layer of rice across the entire nori sheet
			\item Carefully flip the sheet so the rice faces down
			\item Place several fillings in a line across the center of the nori
			\item Roll tightly from the bottom up
			\item Slice with a sharp, damp knife into 6 even pieces
		\end{itemize}
	\end{itemize}

			\textsc{History of Sushi}
		\end{center}
		\rule{\linewidth}{0.5mm}
	}

	The \alert{word sushi} comes from the Japanese phrase \enquote{su} (vinegar) and \enquote{shi} (rice), referring to vinegared rice that is combined with ingredients like raw or cooked fish, vegetables, or egg.

	Sushi originated over a \alert{thousand years ago} in Southeast Asia as a method of preserving fish by fermenting it with rice. This early form, called narezushi, allowed people to store fish for months. When it reached Japan around the 8th century, it gradually evolved. Eventually, the Japanese started eating the rice along with the fish and cutting the fermentation time down to a brief souring rather than months-long preservation.

	By the \alert{Edo period} (1603–1868; named for Edo, the Tokugawa shogunate’s capital, following the custom of naming eras after the ruling seat; the city was renamed Tokyo when the imperial court moved in 1868), a faster, fresher form of sushi emerged: edomae zushi. This style featured raw or cured fish atop small rice balls and was sold as street food. It became the prototype for today’s nigiri sushi.

	When sushi began to evolve from narezushi (fermented fish) to edomae sushi, chefs began experimenting with portable forms of the dish. \alert{Rolled sushi} (maki-zushi) became popular as it was easy to eat on the go. Nori sheets were the perfect tool for this: they held everything together and added a clean, ocean-like savor that complemented the rice and fish.

	\alert{Nori} has been eaten in Japan for over a thousand years, but it wasn't always in sheet form. Originally, it was scraped from rocks in tidal areas and sold as a paste. Around the 1700s, during the Edo period, Japanese food producers developed a method to dry and shape seaweed into paper-like sheets, inspired by traditional paper-making techniques. This innovation made it much easier to store, handle, and use nori in new ways.
	% (nori-tsukudani)

	Western sushi, especially in places like the U.S., has taken creative liberties with traditional forms. Rolls like the \alert{California Roll} (with imitation crab, avocado, and cucumber) were created in Los Angeles in the 1960s, likely by chef Ichiro Mashita to appeal to Western tastes and avoid raw fish. The \alert{Dragon Roll} is another modern creation, usually filled with eel and cucumber and topped with avocado to resemble dragon scales.

	\alert{Traditional Japanese sushi} emphasizes balance, seasonality, and simplicity. Raw fish is served in delicate slices, highlighting the freshness and natural flavor. Rice is subtly seasoned, and garnishes like wasabi, soy sauce, or pickled ginger are used sparingly.

	\alert{Western sushi}, particularly in the U.S., Canada, and Europe, tends to be more fusion-oriented. It includes non-traditional ingredients like avocado, cream cheese, spicy mayonnaise, and fried elements. Rolls are often large, richly flavored, and visually elaborate. This adaptation helped sushi become more accessible to Western palates.

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \alert{Nigiri:}
		The word \textit{nigiri} comes from the Japanese verb \textit{nigiru}, meaning \enquote{to grasp} or to \enquote{squeeze with the hand}. It refers to how the sushi is hand-pressed into a small oval shape.
		A piece of sushi rice is formed by hand and topped with a slice of fish. Some nigiri is lightly brushed with soy sauce or wasabi.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \alert{Onigiri:}
			The word \textit{onigiri} also stems from \textit{nigiru}; the leading \enquote{o} is an honorific/politeness prefix. Unlike nigiri, which uses vinegared sushi rice as a base for a topping, onigiri is plain, lightly salted rice pressed into a portable triangle or cylinder and often partially wrapped in nori to keep hands clean. Onigiri is a rice snack (not sushi) meant to be eaten by hand; nigiri is sushi and typically eaten with chopsticks.
		\end{itemize}

		\item \alert{Maki – Rolled Sushi:}
		\textit{Maki} comes from the verb \textit{maku}, which means \enquote{to roll}. Maki sushi is rolled using a bamboo mat (\textit{makisu}) and wrapped in seaweed (\textit{nori}). It is then cut into bite-sized pieces.
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \alert{Hosomaki:}
			The word \textit{hosoi} means \enquote{thin}, and \textit{maki} means \enquote{roll}, so \textit{hosomaki} is a \enquote{thin roll}. It contains one simple filling wrapped in nori with rice inside.

			\item \alert{Futomaki:}
			\textit{Futoi} means \enquote{fat} or \enquote{thick}, so \textit{futomaki} is a \enquote{thick roll}. It includes multiple colorful fillings, vegetables, cooked ingredients, creating a rich and decorative look.
		\end{itemize}

		\item \alert{Uramaki – Inside-out Roll:}
		\textit{Ura} means \enquote{reverse} or \enquote{inside-out}, so \textit{uramaki} is a \enquote{reverse roll}. This Western-invented style has the rice on the outside and nori hidden inside. Uramaki often includes sesame seeds, fish roe, or sauces on the outside, offering a more complex taste and visual appeal.

		\item \alert{Gimbap – Korean Seaweed Rice Roll:}
		\textit{Gim} means seaweed and \textit{bap} means rice in Korean. Like maki, it is rolled in seaweed, but the rice is seasoned with sesame oil (not vinegar), and fillings are cooked or pickled. It is picnic and street food in Korea and not served with soy-wasabi dipping like sushi.
	\end{itemize}

	\section*{Notes}

	\begin{itemize}
		\item \alert{Rice preperation:} rinse until water runs mostly clear (it should still be a little bit cloudy), then soak the measured rice in its cooking water: 20-30 minutes in the rice-cooker bowl before starting, or about 30-40 minutes if cooking on the stove so grains hydrate evenly; skipping the soak makes the rice noticeably less sticky, and skipping the rinse leaves starch that stops the grains from absorbing sushi-zu evenly.
		\item \alert{Fillings:} you can use typical sushi roll fillings (e.g., salmon, tuna-mayo, pickled veg, avocado, cucumber) either in the center (\textit{naka-dane} onigiri) or mixed into the rice (\textit{mazemai} onigiri).
		\item \alert{Serving:} wrap nori just before eating for crispness.
		\item \alert{Resting:} cover shaped onigiri with a clean towel so they do not dry out.
	\end{itemize}

	\subsection*{History and Facts}
	\alert{Onigiri} goes back at least to the \alert{Heian period} (794–1185; named after the new capital city Heian-kyo, present-day Kyoto, meaning \enquote{peace and tranquility}, because the imperial court moved there and the era took its name from the seat of power) as portable salted rice. Samurai carried them for quick meals; the salt and preserved fillings (like umeboshi, salted pickled plum that is very sour-salty and keeps well) kept rice safe. The triangular shape stacks well in \alert{bento} (portable boxed meals; \textit{bento} comes from a Chinese word for \enquote{convenient} and spread in Japan as a handy packed lunch) and is easy to grip. Unlike seasoned sushi rice, onigiri uses plain, lightly salted rice. %Modern convenience-store wraps keep nori crisp until opening.

	\vspace{0.15cm}
	\subsection*{Furikake Purpose and Name}
	\alert{Furikake} means \enquote{sprinkle over}, it combines \textit{furi} (to sprinkle/scatter) + \textit{kake} from \textit{kakeru} (to apply/pour on). It was created to boost flavor and nutrition on plain rice. Its \alert{umami} comes from naturally occurring glutamates (the salt form of glutamic acid) in the dried fish (katsuobushi), seaweed, and toasted sesame, plus nucleotides especially from the fish and seaweed. % It is sprinkled inside or outside onigiri to add savory punch without moisture.

	\vspace{0.15cm}
	\subsection*{Umami Across Cultures}
	Across cuisines, cooks reach for fermented, dried, or slow-cooked ingredients to add savory depth. \alert{Japan} leans on dried seaweed, smoked fish flakes, soy-based seasonings, and mushroom broths. Chinese cooking layers fermented soy pastes and fermented black beans with dried mushrooms and seafood. \alert{India} often uses concentrated tomato, fermented vegetables, and aged dairy for body, while \alert{Nepalese} stews lean on dried greens or soy to enrich broths. \alert{Western kitchens} get the same effect from tomato paste, parmesan, anchovies, Worcestershire sauce, sun-dried tomatoes, mushrooms, or stock cubes (compressed bouillon of dehydrated stock, salt, fat, monosodium glutamate/yeast extract, and aromatics).

	Our taste for \alert{umami} is an evolutionary cue for protein-rich, amino acid–dense foods, so these ingredients naturally feel satisfying.
